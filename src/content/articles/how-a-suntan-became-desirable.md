A tan is a wound healing in slow motion. Pigment is the skin's response to
damage — [the whole point of a tan is defense](/articles/how-a-tan-protects-skin/),
a shield thrown up after the UV has already landed. For most of recorded
history, people understood this well enough to arrange their entire lives
around avoiding it. Then, in the space of a single decade, the most desirable
thing a fair-skinned person could do was go out and get one.

That reversal is the reason there was ever a market for a tanning drug at all.
It is worth understanding how completely the meaning of a tan flipped.

## When pale was the luxury

For centuries the status logic ran the other way, and it was rigid. If your
skin was pale, it announced that you did not labor outdoors — that you were
wealthy enough to stay indoors, shaded, idle. If your skin was brown, it
marked you as someone who worked in fields under the sun. Pale was the
aristocrat's colour. Parasols, long gloves, wide hats, and frankly toxic
lead-based whiteners were all technologies in service of the same message: *I
do not have to work in the light.*

## The decade it inverted

In 1923, the couturier Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel spent too long in the sun on a
cruise to Cannes, and the press ran photographs of her stepping off the boat
noticeably bronzed. It landed at exactly the moment the underlying economics
had shifted. The Industrial Revolution had moved work *indoors* — into
factories and offices — so the people now stuck out of the sun were the
labouring classes, and the people who could lie on a beach at Cap d'Antibes
were the ones with money and time. Almost overnight, a tan stopped meaning
*fieldwork* and started meaning *the Riviera*.

The fashion press made it a rule with startling speed. By its June 1929 issue,
*Harper's Bazaar* was informing readers that "if you haven't a tanned look
about you, you aren't part of the rage of the moment." Within a few years a
shield against the sun had been rebranded as proof you could afford to sit in
it.

## Industrialising the tan

A desire that strong pulls an industry into being to serve it. First came the
sun-worship of the mid-century beach — oils and creams to accelerate the very
process dermatology would later spend decades warning against. Then came the
machine that severed the tan from the weather entirely.

In the early 1970s a German entrepreneur named Friedrich Wolff was studying
the effects of UV light on athletes and noticed a side effect worth more than
the sport science: it browned the skin. He and his brother turned the
observation into a product, founding the indoor-tanning industry in 1975; the
sunbed reached the United States by 1979. You no longer needed the Riviera, or
even summer. The tan had become a year-round consumer good you could buy in a
strip mall.

## The irony that closes the loop

And then the science caught up, and it was not kind to the fashion. In 2009 the
World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer placed
UV-emitting tanning devices in **Group 1** — "carcinogenic to humans," the same
category as tobacco and asbestos. Starting sunbed use before age 30 is
associated with roughly a 75% increase in melanoma risk. The thing that had
come to signal health, wealth, and leisure was, on the evidence, a documented
carcinogen.

Hold those two facts together, because their collision is the whole setup for
what follows on this site. A century of culture had made a tan close to
non-negotiable for an enormous number of people — and the ordinary way to get
one had just been confirmed to cause the deadliest skin cancer. That is the
precise gap a "tan without the sun" was invented to fill. The desire was
manufactured by fashion; the danger was certified by science; and into the
space between them walked
[the research that tried to build a safer tan](/articles/where-melanotan-came-from/) —
and, when that research escaped the lab,
[a cosmetic that went underground](/articles/why-a-cosmetic-went-underground/).
