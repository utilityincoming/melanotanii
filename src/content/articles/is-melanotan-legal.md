It is the first question anyone asks about the drug, and the honest answer
fits in one sentence: **Melanotan II is not legal to sell anywhere, and no
regulator has approved it for anything.** Everything after that is detail
about verbs and borders.

The reason the question keeps getting asked is that three different
questions are packed into it. *Is it an approved medicine?* Nowhere — not
in the US, UK, EU, Australia, Canada, or anywhere else. *Is selling it
illegal?* Yes, in every major market: unapproved new drug in the US,
unauthorised medicinal product in the UK and Ireland and across the EU,
and prohibited supply under Australia's Therapeutic Goods Act. *Is having
it illegal?* Mostly grey, with one hard exception — Australia, where
melanotan sits in Schedule 9 of the Poisons Standard, the schedule
reserved for prohibited substances, which makes possession itself an
offence.

## The one approved exception — and why it isn't one

"Melanotan" without a number is ambiguous, and the ambiguity does real
work. **Melanotan I**, the earlier compound, became afamelanotide — the
drug now marketed as [Scenesse](/articles/scenesse/), an implant approved
in the EU, US and Australia for a rare genetic disorder in which sunlight
causes burning pain. It is a prescription medicine, delivered by
specialists, for a narrow disease. **Melanotan II** is the peptide sold
online for tanning, and it has never been approved anywhere. The approved
cousin gets cited as proof the gray-market vial is somehow legitimate. It
isn't; the two compounds differ, and literally no label on earth covers a
bottle bought from a website.

The approving agencies themselves say this plainly. The FDA, the UK's
MHRA, Ireland's HPRA, and Australia's TGA have each warned against the
unapproved products while holding the approved implant at
arm's length from them.

## Who got punished, and for what

Legality is abstract until someone is fined. The record so far runs the
way the [enforcement map](/articles/the-enforcement-map/) lays it out: the
UK suspended more than a hundred trading websites; Ireland ordered over
500 social-media and marketplace ads removed between 2020 and 2022;
Australia issued A$101,412 in infringement notices to a single supplier,
paid in May 2026. In every case the target was the *seller*. Personal
buyers outside Australia have rarely faced charges — which is a
description of enforcement priorities, not a legal status. "Nobody is
prosecuting buyers" is not the same sentence as "it is legal."

## The question underneath the question

Most people searching this are really asking: *if it were dangerous or
banned, could I even be looking at it?* The answer is yes. The market
exists precisely because a borderless, platform-hosted trade can outrun
border-bound agencies — the legal status was never in doubt; the
enforcement capacity is what lags. For the jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction
detail, kept current as agencies act, the standing reference is the
[regulatory tracker](/regulatory/). For how the market holds anyway,
start with [how it's sold now](/articles/how-its-sold-now/).
