The certificate arrives with the reassuring vocabulary of a laboratory: a large
percentage, a colored graph, a test date, perhaps a badge promising that the
product is *independently verified*. The number does not have to say “safe.”
Its job is to make your brain finish the sentence.

**“99% pure” is not a clinical verdict.** Without its method, impurity profile
and batch identity, the claim leaves open what was measured and what that
measurement can support. The label *lab tested* can describe a genuine analysis
and still leave the questions that matter to a person using the product
unanswered. This is not an argument that laboratories cannot measure
peptides. It is an argument about the distance between a measurement and a
medicine.

## The sample is the first limit

A report describes the material that reached the laboratory. Before its
numbers can mean anything about a consumer's container, someone has to connect
that sample to a production batch, to the containers filled from it, and to
what actually shipped.

That connection is not created by printing a lot number under a chart. The
chain of custody matters: who selected the sample, what it represented, and
whether the laboratory had any role beyond receiving it. A confident PDF from a
real laboratory can still answer a much smaller question than the sales page
asks it to settle.

Compare that with the development of an approved medicine. The [ICH quality
guideline](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/ich-q-6-test-procedures-and-acceptance-criteria-new-drug-substances-and-new-drug-products-chemical-substances-step-5_en.pdf)
describes specifications — tests, procedures and acceptance criteria — as one
part of a larger control strategy: characterised material, suitable facilities,
validated manufacturing, raw-material testing, stability work and a controlled
manufacturing process. One percentage is not the system.

This is why a “lab tested” badge should not be read as an approval stamp. No
regulator has approved Melanotan II as a tanning medicine because somebody
published a certificate for it.

## Five questions the magic number hides

**Identity: what was in the measured sample?** Checking purity and proving
identity are not the same job. An identity claim needs a method and reference
suited to that question. The
[2012 systemic-toxicity case](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23121206/)
makes the distinction concrete: investigators used mass spectrometry against a
purchased standard to confirm Melanotan II. That was meaningful evidence for
that case. It was not a route to certifying an entire market.

**Purity: a proportion of what?** Synthesis can produce related impurities.
Water, salts and other non-peptide material can also occupy part of a
container. A percentage has to be read with its method, impurity profile and
what was actually included in the denominator. Without that context, a bigger
number is not automatically a better safety answer.

**Content: how much is in this container?** Purity and quantity are not
synonyms. A sample can be described mostly in terms of a target molecule while
the finished container's actual fill remains a different question. This is not
a semantic quibble: for a systemic drug, the amount in the product matters.

For the Melanotan II product they actually tested, Australia's TGA made that
distinction tangible. Investigators seized **five nasal-spray bottles labelled
“Pure Tans Triple Strength 30 MG.”** Testing confirmed Melanotan II, but
estimated [22 to 54 mg across the
bottles](https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/safety-monitoring-and-information/safety-alerts/melanotan-ii-tanning-peptide-products-found-be-inconsistently-dosed),
based on their labelled 20 mL volume. Identification did not make the printed
amount consistent.

**Sterility: is the finished preparation free from viable microorganisms?**
That requires separate microbiological assessment, with suitable processes and
controls. A chemical analysis does not automatically supply that answer. The
[ICH guidance](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/ich-q-6-test-procedures-and-acceptance-criteria-new-drug-substances-and-new-drug-products-chemical-substances-step-5_en.pdf)
sets out specific sterility and endotoxin tests for parenteral new-drug
products. That is what the approved-medicine specification has to address; it
is not a finding that this unapproved market has those controls.

**Handling: what happened after the instrument run?** A sealed, recorded
manufacturing process and a shelf-life programme are not the same as a
powder moved through an opaque reselling chain. A result captured on one day is
not a permanent description of everything subsequently shipped under the same
picture.

## What a broader assay can still miss

A study of **a different peptide**, semaglutide, illustrates why the questions
have to stay separate. In [Ashraf and colleagues' 2024
investigation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11582493/), three
vials bought from illegal online sellers contained no viable microorganisms at
the time of testing. Yet bacterial endotoxin — a different hazard — was
detected in every vial. Measured purity was 7.7% to 14.37%, against 99% claims,
and the semaglutide content exceeded the labelled amounts by 28.56% to 38.69%.

That study is not a survey of Melanotan II vials. It must not be turned into a
pretend frequency estimate for them. Its value is as a controlled, analytical
demonstration of the category error: a purity-only story can miss content,
microbiology and endotoxin, and those omissions matter.

A [Belgian screening of falsified peptide
drugs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30029448/), sampled from three suspected
illegal internet pharmacies, found variations in unit content, peptide-related
impurities, and elemental contamination. It tested other peptide and
polypeptide drugs, not Melanotan II, and cannot estimate what fraction of the
whole market contains them. It shows why a generic “peptide” claim is not a product-quality
investigation.

The reasonable conclusion is neither that every certificate is forged nor that
every vial contains poison. It is that a seller cannot substitute one attractive
number for the strength and quality controls of an approved finished medicine.

## The last gap is clinical

Imagine the strongest possible version of the paperwork: a genuine sample,
handled correctly, tested with suitable methods, traced to the right
containers. There is still no clinical conclusion about chronic Melanotan II
use in that container. The chemical report does not assess melanocortin's
whole-body effects, the uncertain long-term record, or the changes in moles
that warrant medical attention. Those belong on [the side-effect
ledger](/articles/the-side-effect-ledger/) and in [the mole
problem](/articles/the-mole-problem/).

Testing is not a ritual cancellation of pharmacology. A more carefully measured
agonist would still be a systemic, non-selective agonist.

The gray market's trick is the size of the inference. An analysis has a sample,
a batch and a set of methods. A clinical product has a whole controlled
history. Presenting the first as proof of the second sells the certificate's
wording and skips the chain between it, the container and the person.... truncated by the context compressor...
