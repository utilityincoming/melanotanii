The gray market talks about Melanotan II as if "strong" were one word. It
isn't. Tanning pharmacology turns on three levers, and the sales pitch blurs
them. Separating them is the only way the phrase "super-potent agonist" means
anything — and the only way to see which property a real drug would have to
add, and which this compound already has too much of.

The hormone being amplified is
[α-MSH](/articles/the-misnamed-hormone/): a local, minutes-long message the
skin makes after ultraviolet damage. Everything below is what the Arizona
chemists did *to* that message. None of it is a dose, a schedule, or a route.

## Potency: how little it takes to fire the lock

Potency is not "how dark you get." It is how little of a molecule it takes to
switch a receptor on.

The receptor reads a four-residue stretch in the middle of α-MSH,
His-Phe-Arg-Trp. That stretch is the message. The rest of the peptide is
scaffolding that holds the message in a shape the receptor can recognise, and
that scaffolding is fragile: the natural phenylalanine at position 7 sits in
the L-form, the form the body's own enzymes expect, and they cut there.

Swap that L-phenylalanine for its mirror image, D-phenylalanine, and two
things happen at once. The message is held in the shape that fits the
receptor, and the enzymes that chew L-peptides don't recognise the cut site.
That is potency as a chemist means it: a key that still fits, and that is
harder to snap. The assay that could see this, years before anyone cloned the
receptor, was a piece of frog skin going dark
([how the molecule was built in the dark](/articles/before-the-receptor/)).

A useful lab word for the same idea is the EC₅₀: the concentration that, in a
dish, produces half of the maximum response. A lower EC₅₀ means a more potent
compound *in that assay*. It is a comparison between molecules. It is not a
human dose, and it cannot be turned into one. Anyone selling a number from an
EC₅₀ table as a protocol is changing the subject.

## Super-potency: the jump over the hormone itself

Super-potency is potency measured against the natural signal, and found to be
in another league.

In 1980 Sawyer, Hruby, Hadley and colleagues published
[Nle⁴, D-Phe⁷]α-MSH — norleucine in place of the oxidation-prone methionine,
D-phenylalanine in place of L-phenylalanine. In the adenylate-cyclase assay,
the cell's own "receptor fired" readout, that peptide ran about 26 times
α-MSH. On frog skin it darkened pigment at a small fraction of the
concentration the natural hormone needed. They called it superpotent and
ultralong. Melanotan I is that peptide, later approved as afamelanotide for a
rare disease. Melanotan II is the cyclic descendant: same message, tied into
a ring so the shape cannot unfold.

Read the 26-times figure as what it is: one assay, one paper, amphibian and
cell-free pharmacology from 1980. It explains why the chemists knew they had
beaten the natural hormone before the receptor had a name
([Mountjoy and colleagues cloned the melanocortin receptors only in
1992](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1325670)). It does not say
how a person should use anything. A number from a frog skin is not a number
from a person.

## Relentless: the signal that will not clock off

The third lever is time, and it is the one the body was most careful about.

Natural α-MSH is cleared in minutes. That is the control system. Two further
edits defeat it. Norleucine at position 4 replaces a methionine that oxidises
and falls apart. The D-phenylalanine resists the proteases aimed at the
message. Cyclisation — stitching the chain into the ring that Melanotan II
is — keeps the shape from flopping open, which is another way a peptide gets
destroyed. The result is not a louder whisper. It is a whisper that does not
stop when the body intended it to.

For a tan, the variable that matters is exposure over time, not a single peak.
Pigment is a downstream product: the receptor stays occupied, cAMP stays up,
the melanocyte keeps making melanin, and the colour outlasts the peptide. That
fan of clocks — minutes for the hormone, hours for the analog, weeks for the
pigment — is the pharmacokinetic story in
[a drug with no address](/articles/a-drug-with-no-address/). This page only
needs the distinction: duration is not potency. A brief, potent pulse and a
long, modest one are different drugs. Melanotan II is the long one, on
purpose.

## The lever that was never pulled

None of those three chooses a receptor.

α-MSH's message fits more than MC1R. A systemic, protease-resistant copy
reaches the appetite receptors, the arousal receptors, and the receptors in
the brain, because nothing in the molecule addresses it to skin. Super-potency
makes that worse, not better: less is required to occupy whatever receptor it
finds, and it finds them for longer. The harms are not a side effect of the
potency. They are the potency, arriving where a local signal was never sent.

Selectivity is the missing fourth word. A photoprotective drug for healthy
people would have to hit MC1R and leave the others alone, and it would have
to prove that the pigment is protection and not only colour. That
specification is
[what a safe tanning drug would actually have to be](/articles/what-a-safe-tanning-drug-requires/).
The gray-market compound is the demonstration of the first three levers
without the fourth. Calling it "strong" is how the pitch hides which lever
was never built.
