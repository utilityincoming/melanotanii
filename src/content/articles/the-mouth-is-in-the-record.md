There are places a tan was never meant to follow you. The inside of your
mouth is one of them.

Yet brown patches on the gums and inner cheeks are now part of the published
Melanotan II record. Not a court-of-public-opinion anecdote: a dental case
report with examination photographs, a drug history, and follow-up after the
patient stopped using the product.

This matters for the person who notices an unfamiliar dark patch and gets two
opposite answers from the internet: *it's just the peptide*, or *it's definitely
cancer*. Neither answer fits the evidence. A drug-associated pigment change is
possible; a specific pigment change still has to be assessed.

## The mirror did not invent the signal

A [2026 report in *Life*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12942211/)
(PMC12942211; DOI: 10.3390/life16020265) described a 42-year-old man attending
a routine dental visit after taking Melanotan II to deepen his tan. The dentist
found almost symmetrical brown pigmentation on the attached gingiva in both
jaws, together with irregular pigmented areas on the lining of both cheeks.

**Gingiva is the medical name for gums. Buccal mucosa is the lining of the
cheeks.** Those were the sites in this case. The patient's lips, tongue and
palate showed no pigmentation at that examination.

That is worth stating precisely, because a search for "Melanotan lips" can
otherwise manufacture a documented symptom the paper did not report. The
relevant clinical sign here was pigmentation inside the mouth, not a universal
list of what every Melanotan user should expect.

The patient reported both tobacco use and regular sunbed use. Some gum
pigmentation had also been noted at an earlier dental visit two years before
the reported Melanotan II exposure; the distribution and intensity had since
changed. Those details make his history less tidy than a slogan. They do not
erase the documented association. They show why an accurate medical history is
part of the evidence rather than a conversational extra.

## One lesion, two different questions

The drug's cosmetic proposition concerns skin. Skin pigment cells are not,
however, the only melanocytes a person has. Melanocytes also occur in oral
mucosal tissue. The case authors describe a plausible mechanism in which
MC1R stimulation on oral melanocytes increases melanin production and transfer
to neighbouring cells — consistent with the
[non-selective, whole-body signal](/articles/one-hormone-five-receptors/) the
molecule delivers.

That mechanistic explanation is a way to understand an observation, not
proof of a particular patient's diagnosis. There are other causes of brown,
blue-grey or black patches in the mouth. A [clinical review of pigmented oral
lesions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40369390/) lists physiological
pigmentation, post-inflammatory pigmentation, smoker's melanosis, an amalgam
tattoo, benign pigmented lesions and malignant conditions among the
possibilities.
These can look similar even though their clinical significance is very
different.

So the useful split is:

- **Could the product be involved?** Yes, that is in the published case
  literature.
- **Does appearance prove the product is responsible?** No, not from a
  photograph or from the fact that your skin became darker at the same time.

The second question is the clinician's job. It may involve a dental and
medical history, examination, and — when indicated — a biopsy. This article
cannot turn the appearance of a particular patch into an answer for you.

## The colour can outlast the container

In the new case, after the man stopped the injections, cheek pigmentation had
almost disappeared at the one-month follow-up. At three months the gum
pigmentation was visibly reduced but had not completely resolved. The report's
more detailed clinical table records cheek improvement at 28 days and some
remaining cheek macules at three months: improvement was real, but not the
same for every site.

The article credited above supplies those observations. It does not establish
a typical duration or an “expected outcome” for someone else. Nor can it
separate every contribution from pre-existing gum pigment, tobacco use or
sunbeds. What it establishes is more modest: a person's oral appearance can
remain changed beyond the cosmetic course, and dental follow-up can document
it.

This should not be recast as a waiting period. A new pigmented lesion may
need an examination while it is present, not after a self-imposed countdown
designed to see whether it fades.

That is different from the familiar [mole-darkening
problem](/articles/the-mole-problem/), but the diagnostic caution is the same:
the drug changes the background against which a concerning change is noticed.

## The separate melanoma report deserves neither silence nor a shortcut

There is a second, more alarming case in the mouth-focused literature. A
[peer-reviewed report indexed in 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40210573/)
(DOI: 10.1016/j.ijom.2025.03.014) described a 22-year-old woman who used a
Melanotan II nasal spray and subsequently developed a mass in the upper jaw.
Histological examination diagnosed mucosal malignant melanoma; the abstract
reports surgical removal and ongoing immunotherapy.

**That case is not made up, and it is not proof of causation.** A case report
can put a diagnosis and an exposure in the same clinical record; it cannot
calculate how often the drug causes that diagnosis. The two issues should not
be blended: diffuse brown pigmentation is not the same finding as a malignant
oral tumour, and the presence of one in the literature does not convert the
other into a prediction.

It also does not exonerate a nasal route. Changing the container removes the
needle, not the compound or its product-quality questions. An oral
case associated with a spray does not establish that injection is safer; this
article's pigmentation case involved injections. The useful comparison is not
“which format is safe” but what each report actually records.

## Bring the mouth and the history

A new dark patch, a lump, an ulcer that will not heal or another unexplained
mouth change warrants a dental or medical assessment. It does not have to
hurt before it deserves that attention.

Disclose the product by name, when you used it, alongside any sunbed,
tobacco, medicine and health history, and when you first noticed the change.
“Tanning supplement” is too vague for someone trying to interpret a pigment
change. Bring an earlier photograph when available; use the current one as a
record for the clinician, not as your diagnosis.

The [NHS mouth-symptom guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/mouth-cancer/symptoms/)
specifically advises a doctor or dentist review for an ulcer lasting more than
three weeks, a mouth or lip lump, a red or white patch, or persistent mouth
pain. Those symptoms have many causes and do not automatically mean cancer.
Rapid growth, spontaneous bleeding, ulceration or a firm/raised new lesion
likewise belong in professional hands, not a symptom-comparison thread.

The simplest response is not panic. It is to have what the mirror found
examined, with the whole history on the record.

### Sources consulted

- **Bonchev A (2026).** [Changes in Oral Mucosa Associated with Melanotan II
  Injections: A Case Report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12942211/).
  *Life*, 16(2), 265. Brown pigmentation of the attached gingiva in both jaws,
  plus irregular, bilateral buccal-mucosa pigmentation; some pigment had been
  documented before the exposure. One-month and three-month clinical
  follow-up; remaining pigmentation varied by site. The report explicitly
  frames its mechanism and limitations as those of a single observed case.
- **Wolk R, Massi D, Trochesset D (2025).** [Pigmented Lesions of the Oral
  Mucosa: Clinical Presentation, Histology, and Recommendations for
  Management](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40369390/). *American Journal of
  Clinical Dermatology*. It includes post-inflammatory pigmentation among a broad
  differential of physiologic, reactive, exogenous, benign-neoplastic
  and malignant causes. Careful evaluation, and biopsy when indicated, is
  relevant because appearance alone may not distinguish them.
- **Alsabbagh ASM et al. (2025).** [Melanotan II nasal spray: a possible risk
  factor for oral mucosal malignant melanoma?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40210573/).
  *International Journal of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery*, 54(9):806–808.
  Case report of a diagnosed oral mucosal melanoma in a young woman after
  nasal-spray use. It reports the case and the need for awareness, not a
  causal rate or proof that Melanotan II caused that tumour.
- **NHS.** [Symptoms of mouth cancer](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/mouth-cancer/symptoms/).
  Public-facing escalation guidance for mouth ulcers, lumps, persistent pain
  and red/white patches; stresses both medical assessment and that common symptoms usually have other explanations.
