Of all the costumes the gray market has stitched for Melanotan II, the
nasal spray is the one that sells itself as a mercy. No needle, no
injection, no decision that feels medical. The pitch that rides along
with it is gentler still: a spray, the story goes, lets the colour come
up slowly and evenly — beauty developed in time rather than forced —
where a syringe is crude and all-at-once. It is a persuasive frame. It
is also backwards. The route that feels gentlest is the one where you
know least about what you actually received.

Start with the molecule, because the route never touches it. Melanotan
II is the same cyclic peptide whether it arrives through a needle or a
nostril: the same unapproved compound, the same non-selective hit across
the [melanocortin receptors](https://melanocortin.com/melanotan), the
same [documented harms](/articles/the-side-effect-ledger/) — nausea,
flushing, blood-pressure swings, and the darkening and change of moles
that gives dermatology its [real objection](/articles/the-mole-problem/).
Changing the packaging changes none of that. A regulator's view follows
the compound, not the delivery: in December 2025 the UK's Department of
Health and Social Care confirmed to Parliament that nasal sprays
containing Melanotan II are unlicensed and unlawful to sell for human
use — the same status the injectable vials carry on
[the regulatory record](/regulatory/).

## "Gentle" is a claim about dose, and the spray can't make it

The idea that a spray produces a slower, safer, more controllable result
is the whole appeal, so it is worth stating plainly what "controllable"
would require: knowing how much drug reached the bloodstream. That is the
one thing trans-mucosal delivery cannot supply.

An injection is many things, but it is quantitatively honest. A measured
volume goes under the skin, and the fraction that reaches circulation is
consistent enough to be studied — which is precisely why every approved
melanocortin drug is delivered by injection or implant, where the dose is
a number a trial can hold still. The nasal lining offers no such
bookkeeping. How much of a peptide crosses the mucosa depends on the
spray's mechanics, the volume that stays put versus the volume that drips
forward or is swallowed, whether the nose is dry or running, and the
molecule's own poor appetite for crossing that barrier intact. The
absorbed amount is not low so much as *unknown*, and unknown in a way that
shifts from one application to the next.

A dose you cannot measure is not a gentle dose. It is an unmonitored one.
"Slow and even" is a description a buyer applies afterward to whatever
result appeared — and the same erratic absorption that can under-deliver
one day can over-deliver the next, which is not gentleness but noise. The
mole-darkening and uneven pigmentation the market files under "developing
in time" are the compound doing to melanocytes what it does regardless of
route; the spray simply removes the buyer's ability to say how much drove
it.

## The vial problem doesn't stay behind with the needle

There is a second thing the syringe was never the source of. The
[vial problem](/articles/whats-in-the-vial/) — no pharmacopeia, no label
verification, no regulator who has seen the batch — belongs to the
supply chain, not the delivery device. A nasal spray from an unknown
vendor is the same untested reconstitution in a different bottle, now
with an *added* layer of uncertainty stacked on top: not just what is in
the liquid, but how much of it ever gets in. Needle-free subtracts the
needle and nothing else.

That is the whole of the myth. The nasal spray answers a real fear — of
the needle, of the moment that feels like a medical act — and answers it
by moving the compound to the one route where the delivered amount is
least knowable. It changes neither the molecule, its unapproved and
unlawful status, nor the harms on the record. It removes only the single
honest number an injection was giving you, and calls the loss a comfort.
